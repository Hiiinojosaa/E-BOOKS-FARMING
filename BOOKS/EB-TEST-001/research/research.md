# Research — Productivity for Beginners (EB-TEST-001, en-US)

Research date: 2026-10-01. Method: web search for the origin and evidence of the core techniques. No marketplace sales data,
search-volume tools or bestseller rankings were available to this agent, so every demand statement below is an ESTIMATE or
HYPOTHESIS, never a FACT.

## Summary

Recommendation: **GO WITH CHANGES** (as a pipeline test it is GO; as a commercial product the niche is crowded and needs a sharper angle).

The topic is evergreen and the core methods are well documented, which makes it a safe subject for factual accuracy. The
weakness is differentiation: "productivity" is one of the most covered non-fiction topics. The angle that best survives the
competition is a *small, complete starter system* for absolute beginners: one capture list, one weekly review, one
prioritization rule and one focus method, set up in a weekend, instead of a survey of dozens of techniques.

## Demand

- ESTIMATE: Demand for beginner productivity content is steady and evergreen, with seasonal peaks around New Year resolutions and the back-to-school/back-to-work period (September). Basis: general pattern of self-improvement topics; not measured here.
- HYPOTHESIS: Readers searching "productivity for beginners" are overwhelmed by advanced systems and want a short, doable starting point rather than a comprehensive manual. To validate with keyword tools before a commercial launch.
- FACT: Classic productivity methods remain widely referenced decades after they were introduced, e.g. the Pomodoro Technique (late 1980s) [S1] and Getting Things Done (2001) [S5], which indicates durable interest in the subject.

## Competition

- FACT: Established, widely known references exist for each building block: Getting Things Done by David Allen [S5], the urgent/important distinction popularized as a 2×2 grid by Stephen Covey in *The 7 Habits of Highly Effective People* (1989) [S3], and Francesco Cirillo's Pomodoro Technique [S1].
- ESTIMATE: Many competing titles are either (a) long, comprehensive systems that intimidate beginners, or (b) short listicles of "50 productivity hacks" with little structure. Basis: general knowledge of the category; not quantified.
- HYPOTHESIS: The gap is a short, warm, step-by-step book that builds *one* minimal system and explicitly tells the reader what to ignore for now.

## Audience

- Primary: adults (roughly 22–45) who feel busy but disorganized, have tried apps or to-do lists that did not stick, and have no prior productivity system. ESTIMATE.
- Secondary: students entering their first job, freelancers starting out, parents returning to work. HYPOTHESIS.
- Pain points (HYPOTHESIS, consistent with how the classic methods frame the problem): too many open loops in their head, everything feels urgent, procrastination on big tasks, starting systems and abandoning them after a week.

## Subtopics and reader questions

- Where do I even start? What is the minimum system?
- How do I stop forgetting things? (capture)
- How do I decide what to do first? (prioritization: urgent vs important [S3])
- How do I actually focus and stop procrastinating? (Pomodoro [S1], if-then plans [S2])
- Why does my brain keep nagging me about unfinished tasks? (unfinished goals and planning [S4])
- How do I keep the system alive after the first week? (weekly review [S5])
- Why do tasks take as long as I give them? (Parkinson's law [S6], used carefully as an observation, not a scientific law)
- Which app should I use? (answer: tool-agnostic; paper is fine)

## Angles and positioning

1. **The weekend starter system** (recommended): build a complete, minimal system in two short sessions; one chapter = one component.
2. "Productivity without apps": paper-first approach. Narrower; could be a later spin-off.
3. "Productivity for overwhelmed beginners": emotional angle, risk of drifting into wellness/mental-health territory (avoid; would raise risk level).

## Title ideas

Note for METADATA: avoid using third-party trademarks or book titles (e.g. "Getting Things Done", "Pomodoro®" as a brand) in the title or subtitle.

## Risks and saturation

- Saturation: HIGH (ESTIMATE). Mitigation: specific angle, practical exercises, honest scope.
- Factual risk: LOW. Main risk is overstating research (e.g. presenting effect sizes as guarantees). Mitigation: describe studies modestly and attribute them [S2][S4].
- Intellectual property: name techniques and credit their creators; do not reproduce the structure or wording of any existing book.
- Health/mental-health drift: keep the book on organization and focus; no claims about anxiety, ADHD or burnout treatment. Risk level stays LOW.

## Sources used

- [S1] Pomodoro Technique — origin, Francesco Cirillo, late 1980s, 25-minute intervals.
- [S2] Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), meta-analysis of implementation intentions: 94 independent studies, medium-to-large effect on goal attainment (d = 0.65).
- [S3] Eisenhower's 1954 urgent/important remark; 2×2 matrix popularized by Covey (1989).
- [S4] Masicampo & Baumeister (2011), "Consider it done!": making a specific plan reduced the intrusive effects of unfulfilled goals.
- [S5] David Allen, *Getting Things Done* (2001): two-minute rule and weekly review.
- [S6] C. Northcote Parkinson, essay in *The Economist* (1955): "work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion".
