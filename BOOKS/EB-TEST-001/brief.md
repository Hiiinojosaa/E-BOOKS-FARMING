# Brief — Productivity for Beginners (en-US)

## Working title
Productivity for Beginners — A Simple Weekend System to Get Organized, Focus Better and Stop Feeling Overwhelmed

## Audience
Adults who feel busy but scattered and have never used a productivity system, or have tried apps and abandoned them within a
week. They are not lazy; they are overloaded with open loops and conflicting advice. They want a short, calm, concrete starting
point. They do not want theory, jargon or a 40-step method. Reading level: general adult, no prior knowledge assumed.

## Promise
By the end of one weekend, the reader has a small, complete system they can keep using:
1. Every commitment is captured in one trusted place instead of their head.
2. Each day starts with a clear, short list of what matters most.
3. A 30-minute weekly review keeps the system alive.

## Differentiation
Competitors either survey dozens of techniques or teach one large, demanding system. This book builds **one minimal system**
from four well-known components (capture, one list, prioritization, focus) and explicitly tells beginners what to ignore for now.
Each chapter ends with a single small exercise. Tool-agnostic: paper or any notes app works.

## Outline
1. Introduction: Why Productivity Advice Fails Beginners — the problem (too many systems, too many open loops); the promise; how to use the book in one weekend.
2. Chapter 1: Get It Out of Your Head — capture everything into one inbox; the brain-dump exercise; the two-minute rule [S5]; why a plan quiets the mental nagging [S4]. Exercise: 20-minute brain dump.
3. Chapter 2: Build One Trusted List — turn the inbox into next actions; projects vs actions; what goes on the calendar vs the list; keeping it small. Exercise: process the inbox.
4. Chapter 3: Decide What Matters First — urgent vs important [S3]; the four quadrants in plain language; the daily top three. Exercise: sort today's list.
5. Chapter 4: Focus in Short Sprints — the Pomodoro Technique [S1]; timeboxing and Parkinson's law [S6]; protecting focus from interruptions. Exercise: two focus sprints.
6. Chapter 5: Make Starting Automatic — procrastination as a starting problem; if-then plans [S2]; making the first step tiny. Exercise: write three if-then plans.
7. Chapter 6: The Weekly Review — the habit that keeps the system alive [S5]; a 30-minute checklist; what to do when you fall off. Exercise: first weekly review.
8. Conclusion: Your Weekend Setup Plan — Saturday/Sunday plan; the minimum to keep; what to add later and what to ignore.

## Tone
Warm, direct, practical, encouraging without hype. Second person ("you"). Short paragraphs. Concrete everyday examples with
clearly hypothetical people (e.g. "Imagine Maya, who…"). No guilt, no "hustle" culture, no exaggerated promises.

## Length
5500 words (about 600–800 per chapter, introduction and conclusion shorter).

## Sources to use
S1 (Pomodoro, Cirillo), S2 (if-then plans meta-analysis), S3 (urgent/important, Eisenhower and Covey), S4 (plans reduce
nagging thoughts), S5 (two-minute rule and weekly review, David Allen), S6 (Parkinson's law). Describe research modestly:
"studies suggest", never "science proves".

## Risk
LOW — organization and focus for general adults. Avoid medical or mental-health claims (anxiety, ADHD, burnout).

## Out of scope
App reviews, team/management productivity, advanced GTD configurations, habit science in depth, mental-health advice.
